## Working interpretation, not proven performance causes

The thumbnail sells a broad desired outcome with very little reading. The title adds an explanatory category, a benefit and comparison with other people. The surrounding activities turn “anything” into recognizable categories instead of relying on a brain image alone. The central figure and connections give the secondary elements a relationship; this is more coherent than a pile of unrelated icons.

The package combines aspiration with a problem. The title/cover offers faster learning; the opening script establishes a concrete failure: a tutorial makes sense while watching, but the viewer cannot perform the first step alone. The script then reframes familiarity versus retrieval. This connects the broad promise to an actual experience and an explanation.

Do not infer that red brains, yellow figures, the word “psychology,” or more peripheral characters caused the view count. Test transferable decisions rather than copying the exact composition.

## Reusable division of labor

- **Thumbnail:** one quickly understood outcome, conflict or transformation, usually expressed with 2–4 large words and one dominant image.
- **Title:** the specific question, explanatory angle or contextual benefit that makes the image worth investigating.
- **Opening:** a concrete scene that fulfills the expectation created by the package, followed by the mechanism/question the film will answer.

Do not require every thumbnail to be benefit-led or every title to start with “Why.” The point is that title and cover contribute different information. Avoid repeating the entire title on the cover.

## Channel-specific application

Keep the channel's recognizable clothed character and readable dark limbs. The established green/teal clothing can continue; yellow bodies and red brain effects are not compulsory. A relevant prop/action or a clean negative-space composition may communicate more than extra characters.

Choose one emotional tension per film: effort without retention, a difficult first move, a phone habit interrupting focus, guilt about resting, or comparison with other people. Give viewers a credible desirable change. Do not label every behavior a disorder, shame the audience, or promise instant intelligence, perfect memory or superiority to everyone else.

Prefer recognizable everyday activities to tiny abstract diagrams. Use metaphorical brain graphics honestly; they are illustrations, not medical evidence. The actual narrated explanation must support the title's claim, and any research claim needs its original source checked.

## Before producing each video

1. Write the audience's concrete problem and the believable outcome in one sentence.
2. Prepare 2–3 title/cover concepts, each coherent with the same actual video.
3. Reduce the cover to one dominant action/subject and a supporting relationship. Add secondary elements only when they explain the promise.
4. Inspect at roughly 160–320px display width: headline, silhouette, contrast and essential objects must survive. Tiny brain texture or interface text is optional detail, not the message.
5. Check that the title and cover contribute complementary information and that the first 15–30 seconds begin delivering it. This is a channel working target, not a universal retention rule.
6. Check identity, spelling, actual platform crops and any duration badge overlap. Ensure the claim matches the film.
7. Evaluate packaging after publication using impressions, CTR by traffic source, opening retention and watch time together. A single low-view result or one competitor's successful thumbnail cannot isolate the cause.

When making long videos, pair this guide with `channel-animation.md`: a strong package sets the promise; the story and acting must deliver it through the full film.
