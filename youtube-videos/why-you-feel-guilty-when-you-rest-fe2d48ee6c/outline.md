# Why You Feel Guilty When You Rest

## Hook

Paragraph one: the protagonist stops working, hears someone coming and reflexively grabs a plate. A three-minute break feels like being caught. Visible question: what exactly is being hidden?

## Open Loop

Paragraph two: nobody accused the protagonist; they volunteered an alibi, and the plate was already clean. Promise: explain why a harmless break needs a defense and show a changed response at the ending.

## Setup

Paragraphs three and four: a new attempt at twenty minutes off becomes cup, trash and drawer. Establish that finishing a chore briefly quiets this character's self-criticism. This is fictional scene interpretation, not a clinical diagnosis.

## Build-up

Paragraph five: daily life keeps generating tasks. Rest conditioned on everything being finished has a moving finish. The retired-clean-kitchen line gives a visual joke without another psychological theory.

## Climax

Paragraphs six through eight: narrow evidence about leisure beliefs, then a qualified self-worth interpretation. The friend message reveals the double standard: another person needs no proof to rest, while the protagonist's own break needs an application. This turn occurs near the middle and adds information rather than restating the opening.

## Payoff

Paragraphs nine through thirteen: distinguish real responsibilities/unfair workloads; ask what would actually go wrong in twenty minutes; leave a specific reminder; choose a genuinely wanted break; allow imperfect comfort without promising a cure.

## Emotional Ending

Paragraphs fourteen and fifteen: the door sound returns, the hand starts toward the same plate, and then returns. The protagonist says they were taking a break. The drawer stays imperfect. Changed action answers the opening; no motivational speech or early subscribe request is added.

Planning runtime: roughly 4:18 at 140 words/minute plus brief purposeful action/reaction holds, targeting 4–5 minutes. Actual timing will be measured after approved audio exists.
